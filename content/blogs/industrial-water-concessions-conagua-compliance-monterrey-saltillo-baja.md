# Water Rights & CONAGUA Concession Playbook for Mexico Manufacturing: Securing Industrial Concessions, Zero Liquid Discharge (ZLD) & Drought Resilience (2026)

In corporate boardrooms evaluating nearshoring expansions to Mexico, physical infrastructure due diligence has historically focused on two variables: industrial real estate lease rates and electrical power drops from the Federal Electricity Commission (CFE). However, in 2026, an equally formidable operational constraint has emerged as a primary risk factor for corporate capital: **industrial water sovereignty and environmental wastewater compliance**.

Over 85% of foreign direct manufacturing investment in Mexico is concentrated across the arid northern border and Bajío states—including Nuevo León, Coahuila, Chihuahua, Sonora, Baja California, and Querétaro. In these manufacturing corridors, severe hydrological deficits have prompted the Mexican federal government, under the leadership of President Claudia Sheinbaum, to implement sweeping regulatory changes. 

The publication of the **Programa Nacional Hídrico (PNH) 2026–2030** in the *Diario Oficial de la Federación*, the replacement of the legacy water rights registry with the **Registro Nacional de Agua para el Bienestar (REPNA)**, and the strict, unexempted enforcement of **NOM-001-SEMARNAT-2021** wastewater standards have fundamentally transformed industrial water management. Multinational corporations can no longer treat water as a routine utility hookup. 

A single permitting error, an unverified concession title, or non-compliant effluent discharge can lead to the Federal Environmental Protection Agency (**PROFEPA**) ordering an **immediate total facility shutdown (*clausura definitiva*)**, stranding tens of millions of dollars in manufacturing capital.

This executive guide delivers a definitive operational, legal, and engineering playbook for Chief Financial Officers, Vice Presidents of Global Operations, Environmental Health and Safety (EHS) Directors, and corporate site-selection committees navigating industrial water acquisition and compliance in Northern Mexico.

### The Federal Statutory Framework: Ley de Aguas Nacionales & Zonas de Veda
Under Article 27 of the Political Constitution of the United Mexican States, all surface water and groundwater resources are the sovereign property of the Nation. Private property ownership of land does **not** convey ownership of the water beneath the subsoil.

The management, allocation, and regulatory policing of water resources are vested exclusively in the National Water Commission (**CONAGUA** - *Comisión Nacional del Agua*), an autonomous administrative branch of the Ministry of the Environment and Natural Resources (**SEMARNAT**), pursuant to the *Ley de Aguas Nacionales* (LAN).

To protect depleted hydrological basins from collapse, the federal executive issues statutory presidential decrees known as **Zonas de Veda** (Prohibition Zones):
* **Veda Total (Absolute Moratorium):** Codified across the industrial submarkets of Monterrey, Saltillo, Tijuana, and Querétaro, a total veda decree makes it legally impossible for CONAGUA to grant new groundwater extraction concessions (*concesiones de extracción de aguas subterráneas*).
* **Legal Implications for Siting:** If an engineering firm or real estate broker claims that an incoming manufacturing facility can "simply drill its own on-site well," **they are misinformed or willfully misleading your investment committee**. Any drilling of an unauthorized well violates Article 118 of the LAN, resulting in federal criminal prosecution, equipment seizure, and immediate facility closure.

### The 3 Legal Pathways to Secure Industrial Water

#### Pathway 1: Acquisition and Transfer of Existing Concessions (REPNA Title Transmission)
Because new concessions are frozen, an expanding manufacturer requiring dedicated deep-well water must purchase an existing, valid concession title from an existing holder (frequently an agricultural cooperative, farm, or retired manufacturing facility) and petition CONAGUA for a **Transmission of Rights and Change of Use (*Transmisión de Derechos y Cambio de Uso*)** pursuant to Articles 29 Bis, 49, and 54 of the LAN.
* **The Approval Window:** This is not an automated commercial transaction. Processing a formal concession transmission through CONAGUA’s regional basin councils (*Organismos de Cuenca*) requires **6 to 12 months** of technical hydrological review.
* **Volume Reduction Audits (*Caducidad*):** Under Article 29 Bis of the LAN, if the historical concession holder failed to exploit the full registered volumetric quota for two consecutive years, CONAGUA automatically applies statutory forfeiture (*caducidad parcial o total*), wiping out the unused volume before approving the transfer.

#### Pathway 2: Municipal Industrial Utility Contracts (SADM, CESPT, CESPE, CEA)
For facilities operating in urban industrial submarkets without private wells, water must be contracted directly from municipal public utilities:
* **Monterrey Metropolitan Area:** *Servicios de Agua y Drenaje de Monterrey* (SADM).
* **Tijuana & Tecate:** *Comisión Estatal de Servicios Públicos de Tijuana* (CESPT).
* **Mexicali:** *Comisión Estatal de Servicios Públicos de Mexicali* (CESPM).
* **Querétaro:** *Comisión Estatal de Aguas de Querétaro* (CEA).
While contracting with a municipal utility eliminates the legal complexity of owning federal water concessions, it exposes the plant to severe operational vulnerabilities: high progressive volumetric tariffs ($4.50 to $8.50 USD per cubic meter) and physical supply curtailments during seasonal summer droughts when municipal water authorities legally prioritize residential neighborhoods over industrial parks.

#### Pathway 3: Master Concessions Inside Institutional Industrial Parks
The most secure operational pathway for mid-to-large manufacturers is leasing within an institutional Class A industrial park (developed by members of AMPIP such as Prologis, FINSA, Vesta, or CPA) that holds an active, legally unencumbered **Master Industrial Water Concession Title**.
* **Lease Covenant Allocation:** The park developer contracts a specific volumetric allocation—measured in cubic meters per month ($m^3/\text{month}$) or liters per second ($L/s$)—directly into the triple-net (NNN) lease covenants.
* **Dedicated Treatment & Loop:** Top-tier parks maintain their own central deep-well extraction network, secondary water treatment facilities, and internal greywater circulation lines (*línea morada*), guaranteeing continuous operational pressure even during regional municipal supply crises.

## The Wastewater Revolution: March 2026 Hard Enforcement of NOM-001-SEMARNAT-2021

<div class="direct-answer" id="direct-answer-wastewater">
<strong>The updated NOM-001-SEMARNAT-2021 standard mandates drastically lower maximum permissible contaminant limits for industrial wastewater discharged into national water bodies and municipal sewers. Enforcing strict thresholds for Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD), true color, acute toxicity, and heavy metals, non-compliance triggers severe CONAGUA fines, operational shutdowns, and concession revocations.</strong>
</div>

While securing water intake is vital, disposing of industrial wastewater effluent represents the most immediate regulatory hazard facing nearshoring manufacturing plants in Mexico.

### The Hard March 2026 Enforcement Cliff
When NOM-001-SEMARNAT-2021 was originally published, SEMARNAT established transitional staging periods for the most complex technological parameters (True Color, DQO, and Acute Toxicity). 

**In March 2026, all transitional relief periods permanently expired.** 

PROFEPA and CONAGUA environmental inspection divisions are actively conducting unannounced physical sampling audits across industrial corridors. The enforcement consequences of non-compliance under Title Eight of the *Ley de Aguas Nacionales* are severe:
1. **Financial Penalties:** Fines ranging from \$35,000 to \$250,000 USD per verified discharge event, escalating daily.
2. **Immediate Physical Drain Closure (*Clausura de Descargas*):** PROFEPA seals the facility’s external discharge outfalls with tamper-evident federal tape, physically halting plant operations within hours.
3. **Revocation of Concession Titles:** Chronic non-compliance results in the permanent cancellation of the facility’s extraction and discharge concessions under Article 29 Bis of the LAN.

## The Zero Liquid Discharge (ZLD) Blueprint: Engineering 95%+ Closed-Loop Industrial Water Recycling

<div class="direct-answer" id="direct-answer-zld">
<strong>Zero Liquid Discharge (ZLD) eliminates wastewater discharge by recovering up to 98% of industrial process water for continuous closed-loop reuse. Utilizing multi-stage membrane filtration (ultrafiltration and high-recovery reverse osmosis), thermal brine concentrators, and mechanical vapor recompression (MVR) crystallizers, ZLD produces pure distillate while converting hazardous liquid effluent into dry solid cake.</strong>
</div>

To achieve absolute immunity from CONAGUA discharge audits, insulate their operations from municipal water rationing, and satisfy corporate ESG mandates, advanced manufacturing operations across Northern Mexico are adopting **Zero Liquid Discharge (ZLD)** systems.

### The Financial Business Case for ZLD in Mexico
While historical executives dismissed ZLD as overly capital-intensive, the 2026 economic equation in Northern Mexico has flipped. ZLD has become an aggressive, value-generating investment:
* **Capex Benchmarks:** Modular, skid-mounted industrial ZLD systems range from **\$800,000 to \$2,500,000 USD** for treatment capacities between $100\text{ m}^3/\text{day}$ and $500\text{ m}^3/\text{day}$.
* **Volumetric Tariff Elimination:** Eliminates up to 90% of municipal freshwater intake tariffs ($85 to $145 MXN per $m^3$ in Monterrey), generating **\$300,000 to \$650,000 USD in direct annual utility OpEx savings**.
* **Zero Discharge Permitting Liability:** By discharging zero liquid effluent into sewers or federal water bodies, the facility eliminates 100% of CONAGUA discharge duties (*Derechos de Descarga* under LFD Art. 223) and completely bypasses NOM-001-SEMARNAT-2021 inspection liability.
* **Payback Horizon:** With avoided tariffs, zero regulatory fines, and uninterrupted production during municipal water rationing, full capital payback is routinely achieved within **3.2 to 4.8 years**.

### 1. Monterrey (Nuevo León): The Apodaca, Pesquería & Santa Catarina Hubs
Following the severe 2022–2024 water crises, Nuevo León completed the 93-kilometer **El Cuchillo II aqueduct**, delivering an additional 5,000 liters per second to the Monterrey metropolitan grid. 

However, *Servicios de Agua y Drenaje de Monterrey* (SADM) enforces strict industrial offtake quotas. Large manufacturing facilities in Apodaca, Ciénega de Flores, and Santa Catarina are required to execute dual-piping connections: receiving fresh municipal water strictly for potable and sanitary use, while contracting treated wastewater (*línea morada*) at \$35–\$55 MXN/$m^3$ for industrial cooling towers, boiler feed, and process lines.

### 2. Saltillo & Ramos Arizpe (Coahuila): The Automotive Heavyweight
Located just 45 minutes west of Monterrey, the Saltillo automotive corridor (home to GM Ramos Arizpe, Stellantis Saltillo Truck Assembly, and hundreds of Tier-1 suppliers) relies almost exclusively on groundwater extracted from deep limestone karst aquifers. 

Because municipal utility *Aguas de Saltillo* (Agsal) has limited distribution in industrial corridors, **over 80% of manufacturing facilities rely on private on-site wells or industrial park master concessions**. 

Water in this region features extreme mineral hardness (often exceeding 600–900 ppm of calcium carbonate and high silica content). Incoming manufacturers must budget significant CapEx for heavy-duty industrial water softening and double-pass reverse osmosis skids to prevent catastrophic scaling in heat exchangers and tooling circuits.

### 3. Tijuana & Mexicali (Baja California): Colorado River Treaty Dynamics
Baja California’s manufacturing ecosystem is defined by international transboundary hydrology governed by the International Boundary and Water Commission (**IBWC / CILA**) under the 1944 U.S.-Mexico Water Treaty:
* **Tijuana:** Tijuana possesses no major natural rivers or abundant freshwater aquifers. Approximately 95% of the city’s water is pumped over the Sierra de Juárez mountains from the Colorado River via the aging *Acueducto Río Colorado-Tijuana* (ARCT). Driven by historic droughts in the Lake Mead basin, the U.S. and Mexico executed **Minute 330**, mandating progressive volumetric cuts in Colorado River water delivered to Baja California. As a result, CESPT industrial water tariffs are the highest in Mexico, frequently reaching **\$8.50 USD per cubic meter**, making internal recycling economically non-negotiable.
* **Mexicali:** In contrast, Mexicali sits directly within the fertile Mexicali Valley, fed by massive gravity irrigation canals (*Distrito de Riego 014*). While volumetric water is abundant compared to Tijuana, industrial expansion faces intense local sociopolitical scrutiny (exemplified by the historical citizen protests that canceled Constellation Brands' \$1.4B USD brewery in 2020). Manufacturers must navigate formal agricultural-to-industrial water swaps under strict state oversight.
