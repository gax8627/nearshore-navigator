The $438.1 billion in duty-free trade between Mexico and the United States is anchored by the legal architecture of the **United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA)**. Unlike passive free-trade zones, USMCA duty-free clearance under **Special Program Indicator (SPI) "S"** is an active, evidence-based customs qualification.

To clear U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) at 0% duty, imported goods must satisfy one of three core legal standards codified in **USMCA Uniform Regulations Article 4.2**:

1. **Wholly Obtained or Produced (Criterion A):** Goods extracted or harvested entirely within North America (mineral resources, agricultural commodities).
2. **Substantial Transformation & Tariff Shift (Criterion B):** Non-originating foreign raw materials must undergo a specified transformation resulting in a change in Harmonized Tariff Schedule (HTSUS) classification (e.g., Chapter, Heading, or Subheading shift).
3. **Regional Value Content (RVC) Thresholds (Criterion C):** A specified minimum percentage of the product's value must be created within the USMCA territory, calculated via either the **Transaction Value (TV)** or **Net Cost (NC)** method:

$$\text{RVC (Net Cost)} = \left( \frac{\text{NC} - \text{VNM}}{\text{NC}} \right) \times 100 \ge \text{Statutory Threshold}$$

Where:
* **$\text{NC}$ (Net Cost):** Total factory production cost minus sales promotion, marketing, royalties, outbound shipping, and non-allowable interest.
* **$\text{VNM}$ (Value of Non-Originating Materials):** Total customs value of components imported from outside the USMCA territory (e.g., China, EU, Japan).

### 1. Sub-Tier Supplier Documentation Failure (42% of Exposure)
In complex assemblies like electronics and automotive systems, Tier 1 manufacturers in Mexico often source Tier 2 and Tier 3 fasteners, brackets, wire harnesses, or sub-assemblies locally. However, if the Mexican supplier cannot provide a legally verified, digitally signed **USMCA Certificate of Origin**, customs law mandates that the component must be treated as **100% Non-Originating Material (VNM)**. This technicality pushes the final assembly below the statutory RVC threshold, disqualifying the entire finished product from preferential treatment.

### 2. Section 232 Steel & Aluminum Melt-and-Pour Mandates (26% of Exposure)
Under USMCA rules, at least 70% of all steel and aluminum purchased by automotive and heavy equipment producers must originate in North America. Following stricter 2025–2026 enforcement guidelines, CBP requires certified mill test certificates confirming that the primary steel was **melted and poured** within the USMCA zone. Importers sourcing Mexican-stamped metal derived from imported Chinese or Russian slabs were assessed standard **25% Section 232 tariffs**.

### 3. Automotive Core Parts Roll-Up Shortfalls Post-ATR Expiration (19% of Exposure)
With the expiration of the Alternative Transition Regimes (ATR), automakers and Tier 1 suppliers can no longer use transitional allowances. Core components (engines, transmissions, suspension systems, EV battery modules) must meet a rigid **75% RVC Net Cost requirement**. Failure on any single core part eliminates roll-up benefits, subjecting high-value components to standard MFN tariffs upon crossing into Laredo or Otay Mesa.

### 4. Administrative "Pass-Through" Shipping (13% of Exposure)
Many fast-growing companies rushed into Mexican contract manufacturing without establishing an authorized IMMEX customs regime. Shipping goods under standard commercial bills of lading rather than USMCA-certified entries resulted in automatic default to Most-Favored-Nation (MFN) tariff schedules.
